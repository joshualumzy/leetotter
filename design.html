System design · v2

Leetotter

A browser companion that watches you solve problems on LeetCode or NeetCode, talks with you by voice, and remembers everything across sessions. It studies you the way a researcher studies a subject, and uses what it learns to choose your next problem and decide how much to help.

9 Oct 2026Goal: master the NeetCode 150 and related problemsPythonSystem design prep comes later
Principles

What shapes every decision

  • Judgment, not rules. No fixed schedules like "redo in 3 days." The model decides what to do next from the evidence it has.
  • A fixed goal, an evolving approach. The goal and how progress is measured stay fixed, and only you change them. Everything about how to get there starts from sensible defaults and is tuned by the agents as they learn about you. Each change comes with a reason in the session wrap-up.
  • Facts are recorded exactly. Everything that happens is logged as it happened. The model's beliefs about you must point back to those records.
  • It doesn't start from zero. You already know a lot. Your past solves and the first few sessions place you; it doesn't teach basics.
  • It remembers how it taught you, not just what you solved, and reuses the framings that worked.
  • Small models get small jobs. Every model call answers one narrow question with good material in front of it.
  • You can always ask "why?" and correct it. Your corrections count as evidence.

Architecture

The system at a glance

The extension is the eyes, ears and mouth. A small service running on your laptop is the brain and the memory. Models are called through your API.

No screen capture is needed. Reading the editor directly gives exact code, every keystroke burst with a timestamp, and test results, at a fraction of the cost of screenshots.

Agents

Two loops: one live, one between sessions

The live loop runs every few seconds while you work. The slow loop runs when you start and when you leave. The event log connects them. One plain piece of code, the coordinator, decides which agent runs when and what context it gets. Agents never talk to each other directly.

Memory

Facts are fixed; judgment is flexible. Only code writes the event log. Only the Reflector changes beliefs about you, and every belief links to the events behind it, so the memory can't drift into fiction.

Keeping memory small as it grows

The raw log grows by about 1 MB per session: code is stored as small diffs every few seconds, audio is never kept (only transcripts), and full model prompts are kept only for recent sessions. A year of regular practice comes to a few hundred MB, on top of a one-time install of about 1 to 2 GB, mostly the speech models. Models never read the raw log beyond the current session. They read the layers above it, so what they see stays the same size after 5 sessions or 500.

  1. Event log: full detail. Read only by code and by the Reflector, one session at a time.
  2. Session summaries: one per session, written by the Reflector, each linking to the events behind it.
  3. Learner model and teaching memory: current beliefs about you. This is what the Planner and Tutor read.

If the log ever gets large, old sessions are compacted: keystroke detail is dropped, while runs, submissions, hints, transcripts, final code and any event cited as evidence are kept.

Problem cards

Background notes, not a script

Every problem has a card: the tutor's background notes, like the notes a human tutor reads before a lesson. The Card builder writes them ahead of time with GLM 5.3 Flash, and every approach on a card is run against test cases. Labels that tests can't check, like complexity and "insight or trick", are reviewed by a second model, qwen3.8:27b. If something on a card seems wrong during a session, you say so and the card is corrected. Cards for the 150 are built in advance; cards for drill problems are built during session setup.

A card holdsWhat it's for
Every reasonable approachEach with time and space complexity, labelled as an insight or a trick, whether interviewers expect it, and how big a jump it is from the next-best one.
Several explanationsDifferent ways into the core idea. Starting points only: your teaching memory overrides them.
Patterns and common mistakesLinks the problem into the pattern map, and helps the Observer recognise known bugs.
Related problemsUnseen problems that drill the same patterns.

How the Tutor uses a card

  • It follows your approach. The Observer works out which approach you're taking, including one that isn't on the card, and the Tutor helps you finish that one.
  • The tests decide whether your code is correct, not how close it is to any reference. New approaches you find are added to the card.
  • Once it works, complexity comes next. If a better approach exists and is worth it, the Tutor offers to work toward it with you.
  • Worth it means real gain. Insights that transfer to unseen problems are pushed and practised. Tricks like KMP are mentioned, explained if you ask, and not drilled. Not knowing a trick doesn't count against mastery unless interviewers commonly expect it. You can always say "teach me anyway".
  • Your way of understanding wins. If a card's explanation doesn't land, the Tutor tries another or builds from how you're thinking. Your own explanation becomes your personal version and is used first next time.
Choosing problems

A map of patterns with your progress on top

The map is built once from the problem cards. Your layer records memory strength for each pattern. The links inform planning: solving a hard topological sort problem also suggests you know BFS.

  • Placement first, and it tells you. Your LeetCode and NeetCode history is imported as a hint of where to look. The first few sessions spread problems across all patterns with light help. The tutor decides when it has seen enough, and tells you. A quick, clean solve moves on immediately.
  • Diagnose or train. Each pick either tests something it's unsure about or drills a known weakness at the right difficulty. The Planner says which, and why.
  • Memory works like memory. Each pattern has a memory strength from FSRS, the well-tested model behind Anki. A clean solve after a gap makes it stronger; cramming adds little; a break makes it rustier without erasing it. Patterns most likely to be rusty get revisited first. That's how revisiting happens without a schedule, whatever your practice rhythm.
  • Fresh problems beat repeats, but repeats have a use. Re-solving a problem you've seen may just be memory of that problem, so only unseen problems count toward memory strength. Old solves make good refreshers, and after a solution was shown, the follow-up is the tutor's call: the same problem a day or more later, an unseen one with the same idea, or a mix. It learns which works for you.
  • Two layers of problems. The NeetCode 150 is the core: mastering it means solving those well and handling unseen problems from the same patterns. Drills come from related LeetCode problems (via "similar questions" and topic tags), with cards built on demand during session setup.
  • Code does the bookkeeping, the model decides. Code computes "weakest", "most likely rusty", "not seen lately"; the model gets a short summary, not the whole graph.
Goal

A deadline without a schedule

You set a goal and a date, for example "master the NeetCode 150 by 31 March". You never commit to hours per week. The tutor learns your actual pace and tells you, in numbers, whether you're on track.

PartHow it works
What "mastered" meansFor every pattern, for both solving and complexity analysis: at least two clean solves of unseen problems, a memory that lasts at least a month, and no failure on the latest attempt. And each of the 150 has been solved at least once in a session without seeing the full solution (hints are fine): a correct solution of your own that passes the tests, even if it isn't the fastest approach. Past solves imported from your account don't count.
Your rhythmLearned from what you actually do: how often you practise, for how long, and how many problems you get through. It describes your pace, it doesn't set one.
ForecastCode simulates your remaining patterns forward with the memory model, using your real pace and how often your solves are clean, and gives a range, e.g. "early April to mid-May". It's wide at first and narrows as it learns. No warnings in the first few weeks, while pace is still noise.
Check-inOnly at the start of a session, never as messages in between. When the forecast slips past your deadline, it shows where you stand and offers concrete options: "move the date by 18 days", "about 4 more problems a week (one extra 45-minute session)", or "focus on the most-tested patterns first".
Planning around itWith plenty of time, plans explore and go deeper. As the deadline nears, they cover unfinished patterns first. If you're ahead, it says so and goes deeper.

Complexity analysis is a skill of its own

  • Tracked separately from solving. You can be strong at writing backtracking code and shaky on why it's O(2n). Your profile shows both.
  • Judged against your approach, not the optimal one. A hash-set solution is correctly analysed as O(n) time and O(n) space. Approaches that aren't on the card are analysed by the tutor and double-checked by qwen3.8:27b.
  • The reasoning counts. "O(n), because each element is pushed and popped at most once" is strong evidence. The right answer with the wrong reason counts as a miss.
  • Asked like an interviewer would. Not after every problem, sometimes only time or only space, and less often for patterns where your analysis is already solid.
  • Analysis concepts are on the map, linked to the patterns where they come up: amortised cost (why a monotonic stack is O(n)), recursion depth as space, hidden Python costs like slicing and in on a list, heap operations at O(log k), backtracking bounds, and whether output counts as space.
Quality

How small models get good enough

  • Prepared background notes. The Tutor never has to solve the problem live. It reads a verified problem card, then follows your approach rather than the card's.
  • Narrow questions. "Is the user stuck?" is reliable on a 9b model. "Watch everything and tutor me" isn't.
  • Structured outputs. Observer, gate and Reflector return structured data that code checks before anything is stored.
  • Personal history in the prompt. The Tutor gets the framings that worked for you on this pattern before, and how much support it took.
  • Slow where it's safe. The Reflector and Planner aren't live, so they can use the biggest model and take their time.
  • A teaching repertoire, not scripts. The Tutor knows how to do a trace, a Socratic question or an analogy well. It chooses by reading the moment; nothing triggers a fixed routine.
  • What you do is the evidence. Test results, how much help you needed, and whether you recall an idea later say more than any rating. When you do explain your idea first, getting it right unprompted is strong evidence you own the intuition.
A session

From "I have an hour" to the wrap-up

  1. You open the panel and say how long you have. The session starts and the tutor starts watching. If you're off track for your deadline, it opens with a short check-in. Then it proposes a few problems, each with a reason. You can swap or skip any of them.
  2. You open a problem with one click. If it's tricky, you might talk your idea through first. If it's easy, you just start.
  3. You code. The Observer watches quietly and wrong turns are left alone. If you're clearly stuck, the Tutor offers help and waits for your yes, then gives just enough to get you moving.
  4. You solve it, or the tutor eventually walks you there. It may ask about complexity, as an interviewer would. Then a one- or two-line wrap-up.
  5. You choose what's next: another problem, a break, one more past your time, or stop.
  6. The session ends when you stop, or after an hour away. A few bullets show what changed in its picture of you and one focus for next time. You can correct it.
